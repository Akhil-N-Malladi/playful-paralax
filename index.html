Probability / lost boarding pass paradox

One wrong seat. Ninety-nine decisions later.

One hundred passengers have assigned seats. Passenger 1 loses their boarding pass and chooses a random seat. Every later passenger takes their own seat if it is free. If it is occupied, they choose a random empty seat. What is the chance Passenger 100 still gets Seat 100?

Choose the answer that feels right
01lost pass
01 02 ··· 99 100
random seat → displaced passenger → random seat → …

After all that randomness, what would you guess for Passenger 100?

Pick the answer that seems most intuitive.

Why can the answer possibly be 1 / 2?

The chaos only matters until the chain of displaced passengers reaches Seat 1 or Seat 100. If someone randomly chooses Seat 1 first, the chain ends and Seat 100 survives. If someone chooses Seat 100 first, Passenger 100 loses it. Those two special seats play symmetric roles in the random chain, so either one is equally likely to be reached first.

How does a model learn? Start somewhere, measure the slope, and take a step downhill. That’s gradient descent. Give it a spin.

A peek under the hood +

The gradient is ∇f = (x, 3y). Each step updates x ← x − αx and y ← y − 3αy, where α is the learning rate. The global minimum is at (0, 0), with zero loss. For this surface, rates below 2/3 converge; larger rates can diverge. The surface is a teaching example, not the weather project’s trained model.

What happens inside the model?

Each day, each path draws an independent standard normal shock z. The model updates the price using geometric Brownian motion: S(t + Δt) = S(t) × exp[(μ − σ²/2)Δt + σ√Δt × z], with Δt = 1/252. The simulation treats 252 trading days as one year, excluding weekends and market holidays.

Drift and volatility stay constant. This demonstration does not include market regimes, jumps, fees, or fundamentals. The paths illustrate uncertainty; they are not forecasts or investment recommendations.

Building on what I learned through UPenn Wharton, I am developing an ensemble of quantitative models focused on healthcare, biomedical, and pharmaceutical stocks. The goal is to combine multiple independent signals into a system with strong predictive power, then use it to identify persistent long-term market inefficiencies that may be overlooked by conventional screening.
